## 1. Editing order

Edit from large decisions to small ones:

1. Verify purpose, facts, scope, and required content.
2. Fix argument, order, omissions, and duplication.
3. Give each paragraph one job and repair transitions.
4. Simplify sentence structure and clarify actors and references.
5. Replace vague or inflated words.
6. Correct grammar, punctuation, spelling, and formatting.
7. Read aloud and perform the format-specific check.

Do not polish a paragraph that should be removed. Do not shorten before identifying required meaning.

## 2. Meaning and evidence

Underline the main claim of each paragraph. Ask what evidence supports it and whether that evidence proves the exact wording. Weaken or qualify claims when support is limited.

Replace vague attribution such as “experts say,” “research shows,” and “it is widely believed” with a named source or remove it. Verify quotations word for word and keep them short. Paraphrase when exact wording is not essential.

Distinguish fact, inference, estimate, forecast, preference, and recommendation. Put uncertainty next to the affected claim. Do not hide it in a closing disclaimer.

Check numerical meaning: unit, scale, denominator, baseline, time period, rounding, and whether relative change conceals the absolute value.

## 3. Reader and purpose

Name the reader in one phrase and the action they should take after reading. Remove content that serves neither understanding nor action.

Do not assume expertise from job title alone. Explain a term when misunderstanding would affect a decision. Skip definitions that the intended reader certainly knows.

Put the most useful information first. Background earns space only when it changes interpretation, trust, or the next step.

## 4. Structure and cohesion

Make the first paragraph answer the central need. Use chronological order for procedures, causal order for explanations, severity order for findings, and decision order for recommendations.

Give each paragraph one controlling idea. Start a new paragraph when the actor, time, subject, claim, or reader task changes. Keep evidence with the claim it supports.

Use explicit transitions for real logical relations: because, but, if, for example, first, then, therefore. Remove transitions that merely signal continued writing.

Keep given information before new information when possible. Repeat the exact technical term rather than introducing an elegant synonym that may look like a new concept.

## 5. Sentences

Put the actor near the action. Prefer finite verbs over noun-heavy constructions: “the team decided” rather than “a decision was made by the team.” Keep nominalization when the concept itself is the subject.

Prefer positive, direct statements. Use negation when the absence or prohibition is the point. Avoid multiple negatives.

Keep modifiers next to what they modify. Make pronoun antecedents unmistakable. Split sentences with several independent claims, nested conditions, or long interruptions.

Use parallel structure in lists and comparisons. Compare like with like. Do not write “Unlike X, Y's cost is lower” when the intended comparison is between costs.

Vary length. A sequence of uniformly short sentences feels abrupt. A sequence of uniformly long sentences burdens working memory. Rhythm should follow emphasis and complexity.

Use passive voice deliberately. It is useful when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, when the result is the established topic, or when tact requires backgrounding the actor without obscuring responsibility.

## 6. Words and terminology

Prefer the shortest familiar word that preserves meaning: use, start, help, many, before. Do not replace a precise technical term with a vague simple word.

Choose concrete nouns and specific verbs. Replace “improve performance” with the measured dimension when known. Replace “address the issue” with the actual action.

Delete intensifiers that lack a scale: very, highly, extremely, incredibly. Replace them with a measurement or remove them.

Avoid clichés and dead metaphors. A familiar phrase can hide the absence of a concrete claim. Use figurative language when it creates a fresh, accurate understanding.

Keep terminology stable. Define an abbreviation once and use it consistently. Avoid jargon, internal names, and needless Anglicisms when the audience may not know them.

## 7. Tone and voice

Specify tone as writing behavior:

1. Directness: immediate answer or contextual lead-in.
2. Formality: everyday language or institutional convention.
3. Warmth: neutral, acknowledging, encouraging, celebratory, or solemn.
4. Confidence: firm conclusion, calibrated probability, or explicit uncertainty.
5. Density: summary, operational detail, or full analysis.
6. Relationship: peer, advisor, service provider, authority, or collaborator.

Match the reader's state. A failure message should be calm and actionable. A security warning should be unambiguous. Bad news should be clear and humane. Celebration may be warmer but should remain specific.

Avoid performative empathy such as “I completely understand how incredibly frustrating this must be” when a brief specific acknowledgment is enough.

## 8. Concision without damage

Cut in this order: throat-clearing, duplicated conclusions, generic context, empty modifiers, nominalized phrases, redundant examples, repeated caveats, and decorative transitions.

Preserve qualifications that change truth, examples that unlock understanding, definitions needed for action, and context needed for trust.

Do not enforce a universal sentence length. Concision means every part contributes, not that every sentence is short.

After cutting, restore any missing logical connector, actor, condition, or next step. Terse fragments are not automatically clear.

## 9. LLM failure patterns

LLM prose often has a statistically recognizable distribution, but no single word proves machine authorship. Diagnose clusters and reader harm.

Common patterns include:

1. Inflated vocabulary where a familiar word is more exact.
2. Noun-heavy, information-dense clauses that sound formal but conceal agency.
3. Generic framing before the actual answer.
4. Repeated stock words, metaphors, and transitions.
5. Formulaic three-part enumerations and mirrored paragraph shapes.
6. Excess headings, labels, bullets, bold text, and summaries for short content.
7. Constant hedging paired with unsupported confidence.
8. False balance and ceremonial caveats that do not affect the decision.
9. Repetition of the prompt, then repetition of the answer in a conclusion.
10. Generic encouragement, praise, reassurance, and offers of further help.
11. Vague citations, fabricated quotations, invented examples presented as facts, and source laundering.
12. Unnatural punctuation density, especially repeated em dashes, semicolons, colons, and parenthetical asides.

Repair the underlying function. Replace inflated importance with evidence. Replace a stock transition with the actual relation or nothing. Replace decorative structure with an order the reader needs. Replace generic warmth with specific acknowledgment or action.

Do not mechanically ban “delve,” “robust,” “crucial,” an em dash, or a three-item list. Each can be correct. Repetition, imprecision, and lack of purpose are the defects.

## 10. Multilingual writing

Follow the target language's idiom, syntax, punctuation, capitalization, and register. Do not calque English sentence order, business jargon, or discourse markers into another language.

For Russian, prefer concrete verbs over chains of verbal nouns, control bureaucratic abstractions, and use a natural word order that keeps subject and action clear. Avoid unnecessary English terms when a familiar Russian equivalent is exact. Preserve established product names and code identifiers.

For English intended for a global audience, prefer short unambiguous sentences, active constructions, consistent terms, explicit references, and literal language. Avoid idioms, culture-bound humor, and phrasal verbs when a simpler single verb works.

Translate tone and function. A polite request, warning, refusal, or legal obligation may need a different grammatical form in the target language to retain the same force.

## 11. Accessibility and scanning

Use descriptive headings only when content needs navigation. Front-load headings and links with meaningful words. Link text should identify the destination or action rather than say “click here.”

Do not encode meaning only through color, position, punctuation, or emoji. Expand unfamiliar abbreviations. Use literal language where cognitive load matters.

Tables work for repeated fields and direct comparison. Prose works for explanation. Numbered lists work for sequence and reference. Avoid complex tables that require horizontal reconstruction.

Write dates, times, units, and instructions so they remain clear across locales and assistive technologies.

## 12. Final quality review

Ask:

1. Does the opening answer the reader's main need?
2. Is every material fact verified and every inference marked?
3. Does each paragraph have one job?
4. Can the reader identify actors, actions, conditions, and next steps?
5. Is any word performing importance, warmth, or expertise without adding meaning?
6. Does the tone fit the stakes and relationship?
7. Is the format justified by the content?
8. Are terminology, names, numbers, dates, and links consistent?
9. Does the text sound natural when read aloud?
10. Can the text stop one sentence earlier without losing value?
