# Visual Direction and Product Character

Use this reference to make visual decisions that belong to the product rather than to a trend template.

## Establish a visual thesis

1. Start from the product promise, audience, operating context, content, brand attributes, and platform. Express the intended character in a short tension such as precise but humane, dense but calm, or editorial but operational.
2. Translate that character into observable choices: type voice, hierarchy, density, color behavior, geometry, image treatment, icon style, material, and motion. Keep these choices mutually reinforcing.
3. Compare at least two credible directions mentally. Select the one that best improves comprehension, trust, task completion, and brand recognition under the actual constraints.
4. Preserve an existing brand and design system before adding novelty. Introduce a new primitive only when existing ones cannot express a verified need.

## Build hierarchy before decoration

1. Let task priority, content meaning, and reading order determine scale, position, contrast, and whitespace.
2. Use a small number of meaningful emphasis tiers. Avoid giving headings, cards, buttons, badges, and illustrations simultaneous prominence.
3. Group by proximity, alignment, shared behavior, and containment. Prefer whitespace, typography, dividers, or a shared background before adding another card.
4. Make one primary action visually dominant within a decision context. Keep secondary and destructive actions distinct without competing for attention.
5. Use color semantically and sparingly. Ensure the interface still communicates structure and state without color alone.

## Apply the removal test

1. Treat gradients, glass, glows, bento grids, cards, pills, oversized display type, illustrations, decorative icons, and motion as motifs that require a reason.
2. Name the product, brand, content, hierarchy, or interaction function of each motif. Remove or simplify it if its absence does not meaningfully reduce comprehension, usability, identity, or feedback.
3. Avoid stacking motifs merely to signal modernity. One controlled expressive system is stronger than several unrelated effects.
4. Do not ban a motif that carries real meaning. A gradient may encode depth or brand energy; a card may define an independent object; motion may preserve spatial continuity. Keep the execution restrained and testable.

## Avoid synthetic product theater

1. Use representative product content, plausible labels, and domain-appropriate data. Mark unavoidable placeholders explicitly.
2. Do not fabricate customer logos, testimonials, usage metrics, ratings, activity, team members, or product capabilities.
3. Do not use decorative charts, random dashboards, generic AI imagery, abstract blobs, or lifestyle photography that has no content role.
4. Do not default to Inter, excessive rounding, soft shadows, violet-blue gradients, or a hero-plus-card-grid composition without a product-specific rationale.
5. Keep standard controls and navigation familiar. Let specificity come from language, content structure, art direction, data, and brand rather than novelty that increases interaction cost.

## Evidence classes

1. Treat WCAG success criteria as normative only within their stated scope.
2. Treat Apple, Material, Fluent, Spectrum, Carbon, GOV.UK, and USWDS guidance as platform or system guidance, not a universal style recipe.
3. Treat Nielsen Norman Group principles as evidence-informed heuristics that require contextual judgment.
4. Treat verified local brand rules, research, analytics, content, and technical constraints as project evidence. Never invent them.
