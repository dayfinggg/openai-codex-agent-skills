# Research

Find the smallest body of strong evidence that can answer the actual question. Prefer traceable primary evidence over source volume, search ranking, or fluent summaries.

## Set the research bar

1. Extract the requested outcome, scope, date range, jurisdiction, audience, and required precision. Resolve only ambiguities that could materially change the answer.
2. Separate quick lookup, ordinary synthesis, and high-consequence research. Increase source diversity and independent verification with uncertainty and consequence, not with answer length.
3. Identify which claims may have changed. Use live retrieval for current, unstable, niche, disputed, version-specific, or unfamiliar facts and whenever the user asks for search or verification.
4. Check the premise before accepting it. Search for evidence that would disprove or narrow the premise when it affects the answer.

## Search deliberately

1. Break the question into answerable claim groups. Search the rarest names, identifiers, quotations, dates, versions, and relationships first.
2. Start with concise queries. Add synonyms, source-language terms, exact phrases, dates, standards or document identifiers, and `site:` or `filetype:` constraints only when they improve precision.
3. Aim initial queries at likely sources of record. Search the open web to discover terminology and counterevidence, then follow citations, references, and links back to the original material.
4. Open and inspect the source. A result title, snippet, generated summary, or another page's citation is a lead, not evidence.
5. Search iteratively. Let useful documents supply better terms, authors, institutions, identifiers, earlier versions, and opposing claims.
6. For each material conclusion, run at least one disconfirming search when the topic is disputed, consequential, or vulnerable to marketing claims.

## Choose sources by claim

1. Prefer the source closest to the fact: official records, statutes, standards, datasets, filings, technical documentation, release notes, original research, direct statements, or contemporaneous reporting.
2. Judge a source by its directness, authority for this claim, evidence and method, currency, independence, correction status, and fit to the requested jurisdiction and version. Do not trust a domain merely because it ranks highly or looks polished.
3. Use secondary sources for orientation, synthesis, independent reporting, or context. Trace decisive claims to primary evidence when it exists.
4. Reject or de-prioritize pages built mainly to capture search traffic: templated or mass-produced summaries, affiliate roundups without testing, copied text, circular citations, vague attribution, missing authorship or dates, topic-mismatched host pages, and pages that do not expose evidence.
5. Do not confuse repetition with corroboration. Syndicated copies, press-release rewrites, and pages citing the same underlying source count as one evidence chain.

## Match the source to the domain

1. For law and policy, use the enacted text, official gazette, court record, regulator, or responsible agency. Verify jurisdiction, effective date, amendments, and current status.
2. For software, use version-matched official documentation, release notes, specifications, source repositories, and package registries. Verify that the cited page applies to the installed or requested version.
3. For science and medicine, inspect the original study or dataset, study design, sample, limitations, and relevant reviews or guidelines. Check registries, corrections, expressions of concern, and retractions.
4. For companies and products, use filings, official specifications, changelogs, status pages, and direct announcements for first-party facts. Use independent testing or reporting for comparative quality claims.
5. For statistics, use the originating dataset and methodology. Check units, definitions, revision schedules, geographic coverage, and whether a figure is preliminary or seasonally adjusted.
6. For news, distinguish when the event occurred from when an article was published or updated. Prefer direct documents and reporters with attributable evidence over aggregation.

## Build an evidence trail

1. Track each material claim with its direct URL, title, publisher or author, publication and update dates when available, relevant passage, and correction or version status.
2. Distinguish what the source states from what you infer. Label an inference and cite the evidence that supports it.
3. Preserve exact names, numbers, units, dates, qualifiers, and quotation wording. Read enough surrounding context to avoid reversing or overstating the source.
4. Classify draft claims as supported, contradicted, or unresolved. Remove unsupported detail. State a limit only when retrieval cannot resolve it.

## Resolve conflicts

1. Confirm that sources address the same entity, definition, period, jurisdiction, version, and measurement method before treating them as contradictory.
2. Build a timeline when information changed. Prefer the current corrected record for present-state questions, but do not assume the newest page is the most authoritative.
3. Prefer direct evidence over commentary and stronger methods over weaker methods. Preserve a material minority or unresolved disagreement when evidence does not justify choosing one side.
4. Explain the concrete reason for the difference, such as a revision, different denominator, scope, methodology, or reporting lag. Do not average incompatible values.

## Cite precisely

1. Cite every non-obvious web-derived claim that matters to the answer. Place the citation immediately after the sentence or tight paragraph it supports.
2. Link to the exact opened page, document, DOI, filing, dataset, or official record. Never invent a citation or cite a search results page as evidence.
3. Ensure citation completeness and precision. The cited passage must support the full claim, and every citation must add support rather than decoration.
4. Quote only when the wording matters. Keep quotations exact and short, and clearly separate quotation from paraphrase.
5. Include only sources actually used. Do not append a long source list when inline citations already make the answer verifiable unless the user requests a bibliography or research log.

## Stop at saturation

1. A quick lookup may stop after one current authoritative source directly answers it.
2. Continue until every material claim has adequate evidence, important dates and versions are checked, conflicts are resolved or disclosed, and high-consequence claims have independent corroboration when available.
3. Stop when successive targeted searches add no new relevant evidence, only duplicate an existing evidence chain, or have lower expected value than answering. Do not chase an arbitrary source count.
4. Resume searching when a credible contradiction, newer authoritative update, unsupported material claim, or promising uncited primary source remains.

## Final audit

1. Verify the answer against the opened sources claim by claim. Check names, dates, figures, units, versions, links, and quotation text.
2. Remove claims whose citations do not entail them. Do not let model confidence substitute for evidence.
3. Answer the requested question directly. Keep facts, inferences, uncertainty, and unresolved conflicts visibly distinct without adding research narration or unrelated advice.
